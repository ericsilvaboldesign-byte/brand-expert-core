## A1 — Peer-reviewed empirical research

### Ehrenberg, Goodhardt & Barwise (1990) — "Double Jeopardy Revisited"
- Andrew S. C. Ehrenberg, Gerald J. Goodhardt, T. Patrick Barwise. *Journal of Marketing*, Vol. 54, No. 3 (July 1990), pp. 82–91. DOI: `10.1177/002224299005400307`.
- Metadata verified via: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002224299005400307
- Supports: the double-jeopardy pattern — smaller brands have both fewer buyers and slightly lower loyalty — across categories and countries.
- Limits: a cross-sectional statistical regularity with documented exceptions (niche and change-of-pace brands, distribution effects), acknowledged in the paper itself. Fader & Schmittlein (*Journal of Marketing Research*, 1993) found high-share brands show "excess" loyalty beyond the DJ baseline. Describes stationary markets; says little by itself about how to grow.

### Lodish et al. (1995) — "How T.V. Advertising Works"
- Leonard M. Lodish, Magid M. Abraham, Stuart Kalmenson, Jeanne Livelsberger, Beth Lubetkin, Bruce Richardson, Mary Ellen Stevens. "How T.V. Advertising Works: A Meta-Analysis of 389 Real World Split Cable T.V. Advertising Experiments." *Journal of Marketing Research*, Vol. 32, No. 2 (May 1995), pp. 125–139. DOI: `10.1177/002224379503200201`.
- Metadata verified via: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002224379503200201
- Supports: advertising's sales effects are real but conditional — increased TV weight alone frequently produced no measurable lift; copy/strategy changes mattered more than weight.
- Limits: US packaged goods, 1980s split-cable panels only; generalization to other categories, media, and eras contested. A formal industry response by Dwight R. Riskey (*JMR* 34(2), 1997, DOI `10.1177/002224379703400209`) challenged aspects of interpretation; revisited by Hu, Lodish & Krieger (*Journal of Advertising Research*, 2007).

### Aaker, J. (1997) — "Dimensions of Brand Personality"
- Jennifer L. Aaker. *Journal of Marketing Research*, Vol. 34, No. 3 (1997), pp. 347–356. DOI: `10.1177/002224379703400304` (original JSTOR DOI `10.2307/3151897`).
- Metadata verified via: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002224379703400304
- Supports: consumers can reliably ascribe personality-like trait structures to brands; the five-dimension scale (sincerity, excitement, competence, sophistication, ruggedness).
- Limits: Azoulay & Kapferer (2003, below) argue the scale conflates brand-identity facets (e.g., "competence" items tap perceived performance, not personality). The five-factor structure failed to fully replicate cross-culturally (Aaker, Benet-Martínez & Garolera, *JPSP* 2001: Japan/Spain yielded different dimensions) and generalizes poorly to individual brands within one category (Austin, Siguaw & Mattila, *Journal of Strategic Marketing*, 2003). Geuens, Weijters & De Wulf (*IJRM*, 2009) built a replacement scale in response.

### Henderson & Cote (1998) — "Guidelines for Selecting or Modifying Logos"
- Pamela W. Henderson, Joseph A. Cote. *Journal of Marketing*, Vol. 62, No. 2 (April 1998), pp. 14–30. DOI: `10.1177/002224299806200202` (original DOI `10.2307/1252158`).
- Metadata verified via: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002224299806200202
- Supports: measurable logo design properties — elaborateness, naturalness, harmony — predict recognition, affect, and perceived meaning; empirically derived selection guidelines by strategic goal.
- Limits: 195 unfamiliar logos, decontextualized lab ratings, single-country (US) sample; real market exposure and brand context out of scope. Cross-cultural follow-ups (Henderson, Cote, Leong & Schmitt, *IJRM* 2003; a 2020 Chilean validation by Araya-Castillo et al.) found only partial generalization.

### Zajonc (1968) — "Attitudinal Effects of Mere Exposure"
- Robert B. Zajonc. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, Monograph Supplement, Vol. 9, No. 2, Pt. 2 (1968), pp. 1–27. DOI: `10.1037/h0025848`.
- Metadata verified via: https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=558111
- Supports: repeated exposure to a stimulus increases liking of it — the foundational mechanism beneath familiarity-based brand preference.
- Limits: Bornstein's meta-analysis of 208 studies (*Psychological Bulletin*, 1989) confirmed the effect but found strong moderators and an inverted-U satiation boundary (overexposure reduces liking), re-confirmed by Montoya et al. (*Psychological Bulletin*, 2017). Strongest effects occur for brief/subliminal exposure to initially neutral stimuli — a narrower condition than typical marketing exposure.

### Klink (2000) — "Creating Brand Names With Meaning: The Use of Sound Symbolism"
- Richard R. Klink. *Marketing Letters*, Vol. 11, No. 1 (2000), pp. 5–20. DOI: `10.1023/A:1008184423824`.
- Metadata verified via: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1008184423824
- Supports: phonetic properties of invented brand names (vowel sounds, consonant types) systematically convey attribute meanings (size, speed, weight, sharpness).
- Limits: fictitious names, forced-choice paired comparisons, student-type samples; measures attribute perception, not choice or purchase — real-market effect sizes not established. Lowrey & Shrum (*Journal of Consumer Research*, 2007): effects depend on the connoted attribute being relevant and positive for the category; later work (Klink & Athaide 2012; Shrum et al. 2012) treats effects as context- and language-dependent.

### Green & Brock (2000) — "The Role of Transportation in the Persuasiveness of Public Narratives"
- Melanie C. Green, Timothy C. Brock. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, Vol. 79, No. 5 (2000), pp. 701–721. DOI: `10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701`.
- Metadata verified via: https://psycnet.apa.org/buy/2000-00920-003
- Supports: narrative "transportation" — absorption into a story — reduces counterarguing and shifts beliefs; the empirical basis for narrative persuasion.
- Limits: student samples reading short literary narratives; transportation was measured more than cleanly induced, leaving parts of the evidence correlational; scale-validity questions on affect items. Van Laer et al.'s meta-analysis (*Journal of Consumer Research*, 2014) finds reliable but **moderate** average effects, varying substantially with storyteller, story, and audience.

### Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman (2004) — "Processing Fluency and Aesthetic Pleasure"
- Rolf Reber, Norbert Schwarz, Piotr Winkielman. "Processing Fluency and Aesthetic Pleasure: Is Beauty in the Perceiver's Processing Experience?" *Personality and Social Psychology Review*, Vol. 8, No. 4 (2004), pp. 364–382. DOI: `10.1207/s15327957pspr0804_3`.
- Metadata verified via: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1207/s15327957pspr0804_3
- Supports: ease of perceptual processing (simplicity, clarity, symmetry, prototypicality) produces liking — the mechanism behind "clean" design being preferred. (A peer-reviewed theoretical review; inherits the limits of underlying studies.)
- Limits: fluency–pleasure is not universal — people sometimes prefer complex, novel, or disfluent stimuli; amended by Graf & Landwehr's pleasure-interest model (*PSPR*, 2015) and Armstrong & Detweiler-Bedell (*Review of General Psychology*, 2008). Effects can be discounted or reversed when ease is attributed to an external source (Bornstein & D'Agostino, 1994).

## A2 — Peer-reviewed / scholarly conceptual work

### Keller (1993) — "Conceptualizing, Measuring, and Managing Customer-Based Brand Equity"
- Kevin Lane Keller. *Journal of Marketing*, Vol. 57, No. 1 (January 1993), pp. 1–22. DOI: `10.1177/002224299305700101` (JSTOR DOI `10.2307/1252054`).
- Metadata verified via: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002224299305700101
- Supports: the customer-based brand equity construct — equity as the differential effect of brand knowledge (awareness + image) on consumer response.
- Limits: a conceptual framework in a refereed venue, not an empirical test; its authority is scholarly acceptance and citation, and its measurement program remains unstandardized across the industry.

### Azoulay & Kapferer (2003) — "Do brand personality scales really measure brand personality?"
- Audrey Azoulay, Jean-Noël Kapferer. *Journal of Brand Management*, Vol. 11, No. 2 (2003), pp. 143–155. DOI: `10.1057/palgrave.bm.2540162`.
- Metadata verified via: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/palgrave.bm.2540162
- Supports: the construct critique of Aaker's 1997 scale (in this corpus it functions as boundary literature).
- Limits: conceptual/definitional, no new data or validated alternative of its own; *Journal of Brand Management* is a lower-impact, practitioner-oriented outlet — a well-cited position paper, not settled empirical evidence.

### Aaker, D. (1991) — *Managing Brand Equity*
- David A. Aaker. *Managing Brand Equity: Capitalizing on the Value of a Brand Name*. The Free Press, 1991. ISBN-13: `9780029001011` (1991 hardcover).
- Metadata verified via: https://www.biblio.com/book/managing-brand-equity-aaker-david/d/1424734219
- Supports: brand equity as a manageable asset with five components (loyalty, awareness, perceived quality, associations, proprietary assets).
- Limits: a scholarly framework, not tested theory; the five components are a taxonomy whose causal links to business results are asserted through cases, not demonstrated.

### Aaker, D. (1996) — *Building Strong Brands*
- David A. Aaker. *Building Strong Brands*. The Free Press, 1996. ISBN-13: `9780029001516` (hardcover; later paperback `9780743232135`).
- Metadata verified via: https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Building-Strong-Brands/David-A-Aaker/9780029001516
- Supports: the Brand Identity Planning model; brand-as-product/organization/person/symbol.
- Limits: same mode as above — an organizing vocabulary validated by adoption, not by evidence; competes with Kapferer's prism with no empirical way to choose between them.

### Keller & Swaminathan — *Strategic Brand Management* (1st ed. 1998; 5th ed. 2019)
- Kevin Lane Keller, Vanitha Swaminathan. *Strategic Brand Management: Building, Measuring, and Managing Brand Equity*. 1st ed. Prentice Hall, 1998 (Keller alone); 5th US ed. Pearson, 2019, ISBN-13: `9780134892498` (5th global ed. `9781292314969`).
- Metadata verified via: https://www.pearson.com/en-us/subject-catalog/p/strategic-brand-management-building-measuring-and-managing-brand-equity/P200000005947/9780135641316
- Supports: the consolidated academic apparatus of brand management — brand resonance pyramid, brand value chain, measurement systems.
- Limits: a textbook synthesis: authoritative as a map of the field's accepted concepts, but its frameworks are pedagogical structures, and case examples are illustrative rather than probative.

### Kapferer — *The New Strategic Brand Management* (2004; 5th ed. 2012)
- Jean-Noël Kapferer. *The New Strategic Brand Management: Advanced Insights and Strategic Thinking*. Kogan Page; "New" title first 2004 (as 3rd ed. of *Strategic Brand Management*, first published 1992); 5th ed. 2012, ISBN-13: `9780749465155`.
- Metadata verified via: https://www.koganpage.com/marketing-communications/the-new-strategic-brand-management-9780749465155
- Supports: the Brand Identity Prism (physique, personality, culture, relationship, reflection, self-image); European school of brand strategy and architecture.
- Limits: the prism is an unfalsified conceptual device; its six facets are not empirically derived and overlap with competing identity models.

### Holt (2004) — *How Brands Become Icons*
- Douglas B. Holt. *How Brands Become Icons: The Principles of Cultural Branding*. Harvard Business School Press, 2004. ISBN-13: `9781578517749`.
- Metadata verified via: https://www.biblio.com/book/how-brands-become-icons-principles-cultural/d/1181474404
- Supports: cultural branding theory — iconic brands perform identity myths that resolve cultural contradictions of their era.
- Limits: retrospective interpretive case method (Coke, Budweiser, Nike...); no predictive validation, and the theory covers the rare iconic tier of brands rather than ordinary brand-building.

## B — Industry-empirical

### Sharp (2010) — *How Brands Grow*
- Byron Sharp. *How Brands Grow: What Marketers Don't Know*. Oxford University Press, 2010. ISBN-13: `9780195573565`.
- Metadata verified via: https://global.oup.com/academic/product/how-brands-grow-9780195573565
- Supports: penetration over loyalty as the growth path; mental and physical availability; skepticism of differentiation, targeting, and loyalty programs. Rests partly on a peer-reviewed substrate (Ehrenberg-school journal work, incl. Ehrenberg et al. 1990 above).
- Limits: relies heavily on Ehrenberg-Bass Institute proprietary panel data and cross-sectional "empirical generalizations". Published counter-positions: Nigel Hollis, "Marketing's Mission: Make it Meaningfully Different" (*Harvard Business Review*, Oct 2013) defends meaningful differentiation; Mark Ritson's *Marketing Week* columns and the 2017 Festival of Marketing debate attack the dismissal of targeting/differentiation; Sharp's reply: "Answering critics" (byronsharp.wordpress.com, Sep 2016). The prescriptive leap from stationary-market regularities to strategy is the contested step.

### Romaniuk & Sharp (2015) — *How Brands Grow: Part 2*
- Jenni Romaniuk, Byron Sharp. *How Brands Grow: Part 2 — Emerging Markets, Services, Durables, New and Luxury Brands*. Oxford University Press (ANZ), 2015, ISBN-13: `9780195596267`; revised ed. 2021 (adds B2B), ISBN-13: `9780190330026`.
- Metadata verified via: https://global.oup.com/academic/product/how-brands-grow-2-revised-edition-9780190330026
- Supports: extension of the growth laws to services, durables, luxury, emerging markets; mental availability measurement; distinctive assets.
- Limits: mixed peer-reviewed reception — Alan Wilson's review (*International Journal of Market Research* 58(6), 2016, pp. 881–882, DOI `10.2501/IJMR-2016-053`) found it weaker than the first book. Inherits the standing Ehrenberg-Bass critiques above.

### Romaniuk (2018) — *Building Distinctive Brand Assets*
- Jenni Romaniuk. *Building Distinctive Brand Assets*. Oxford University Press (ANZ), 2018. ISBN-13: `9780190311506`.
- Metadata verified via: https://global.oup.com/academic/product/building-distinctive-brand-assets-9780190311506
- Supports: the practice layer of distinctiveness — measuring assets on a Fame/Uniqueness grid; building and protecting non-logo brand codes.
- Limits: prescriptions rest on proprietary Ehrenberg-Bass asset-measurement data and on the contested distinctiveness-over-differentiation position (Hollis, Ritson counter-case). No standalone methodological takedown of the book itself was found; critiques attach to the research program.

### Binet & Field (2013) — *The Long and the Short of It*
- Les Binet, Peter Field. *The Long and the Short of It: Balancing Short and Long-Term Marketing Strategies*. IPA, London, 2013. ISBN-13: `9780852941348`.
- Metadata verified via: https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/publications-reports/the-long-and-the-short-of-it-balancing-short-and-long-term-marketing-strategies/
- Supports: brand-building (broad-reach, emotional) drives long-term growth while activation drives short-term sales; the ~60/40 budget heuristic.
- Limits: evidence base is the IPA Effectiveness Awards Databank — a self-selected sample of award entries from mostly large B2C advertisers (selection bias argued explicitly by Harry Guild, BBH Labs, and acknowledged by Binet). Most cases predate the modern digital/performance environment, and 60/40 is routinely over-applied as a universal rule the authors frame as context-dependent.

## C — Practitioner canon

### Ries & Trout (1981) — *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind*
- Al Ries, Jack Trout. McGraw-Hill, 1981. ISBN-13: `9780070652637` (1981 hardcover; 20th Anniversary ed. 2001, `9780071373586`).
- Metadata verified via: https://search.worldcat.org/title/Positioning-:-the-battle-for-your-mind/oclc/6487734
- Supports: positioning discipline — brands compete for a slot in the prospect's mind; own a word; position against the leader.
- Limits: assertion-and-anecdote method; core psychological claims (mental slots, first-mover memory advantage) are stylized, not cited to research; directly contradicted on strategy by the Sharp school (see knowledge map §4).

### Wheeler (& Meyerson) — *Designing Brand Identity* (2003; 6th ed. 2024)
- Alina Wheeler; 6th ed. revised by Rob Meyerson. John Wiley & Sons; 1st ed. 2003 (`9780471213260`); 6th ed. 2024, ISBN-13: `9781119984818`.
- Metadata verified via: https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Designing+Brand+Identity:+A+Comprehensive+Guide+to+the+World+of+Brands+and+Branding,+6th+Edition-p-9781119984825
- Supports: the standard five-phase identity-program process (research → strategy → design → touchpoints → asset management); professional standards of practice.
- Limits: codified craft consensus; no controlled evidence that the process produces better outcomes than alternatives; case library skews to celebrated programs (survivorship).

### Neumeier (2003) — *The Brand Gap*
- Marty Neumeier. *The Brand Gap: How to Bridge the Distance Between Business Strategy and Design*. New Riders, 2003. ISBN-13: `9780735713307` (2005 revised ed. `9780321348104`).
- Metadata verified via: https://www.abebooks.com/9780735713307/Brand-Gap-Bridge-Distance-Business-0735713308/plp
- Supports: brand as "a person's gut feeling"; the strategy–design bridge; five disciplines (differentiate, collaborate, innovate, validate, cultivate).
- Limits: aphoristic practitioner argument; its validation discipline gestures at testing but the book's own claims are untested.

### Neumeier (2006) — *Zag*
- Marty Neumeier. *Zag: The Number One Strategy of High-Performance Brands*. New Riders, 2006. ISBN-13: `9780321426772`.
- Metadata verified via: https://www.peachpit.com/store/zag-the-1-strategy-of-high-performance-brands-9780321426772
- Supports: radical differentiation ("when they zig, zag") as a strategy heuristic; a 17-step process for finding it.
- Limits: rests on the differentiation premise the Sharp school disputes with data; evidence is anecdote and assertion.

### Johnson (2016) — *Branding: In Five and a Half Steps*
- Michael Johnson. Thames & Hudson, 2016. ISBN-13: `9780500518960`.
- Metadata verified via: https://www.thamesandhudsonusa.com/books/branding-in-five-and-a-half-steps-hardcover
- Supports: a leading designer's articulated method — investigation, strategy & narrative, design, implementation, engagement, with the "half step" bridging strategy into design.
- Limits: single-studio (johnson banks) perspective; craft authority only.

### Olins (2003) — *Wally Olins on Brand*
- Wally Olins. Thames & Hudson, 2003. ISBN-13: `9780500511459` (2004 paperback `9780500285152`). Cover styled "Wally Olins: On B®and".
- Metadata verified via: https://www.biblio.com/book/wally-olins-brand-olins-wally/d/1687420102
- Supports: the historical sweep of corporate identity — branding's spread from products to corporations, nations, and culture — from a founding practitioner of the discipline.
- Limits: essayistic and historical; authority is eyewitness practice, not analysis.

## D — Popular / opinion

### Sinek (2009) — *Start with Why*
- Simon Sinek. *Start with Why: How Great Leaders Inspire Everyone to Take Action*. Portfolio (Penguin Group), 2009. ISBN-13: `9781591842804` (2011 paperback `9781591846444`).
- Metadata verified via: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Start_with_Why
- Supports: nothing evidentially. Included because the Golden Circle (why/how/what) is ubiquitous in brand-purpose practice and must be graded.
- Limits: the "limbic brain buys why" neuroscience is not supported by the neuroscience literature; the argument is retrospective storytelling about selected companies (Apple as recurring case). Treated in this corpus as a hypothesis with rhetorical, not evidential, force.

### Miller (2017) — *Building a StoryBrand*
- Donald Miller. *Building a StoryBrand: Clarify Your Message So Customers Will Listen*. HarperCollins Leadership, 2017. ISBN-13: `9780718033323`.
- Metadata verified via: https://www.abebooks.com/9780718033323/Building-StoryBrand-Clarify-Message-Customers-0718033329/plp
- Supports: the SB7 messaging formula (customer as hero, brand as guide) — a usable craft template.
- Limits: no published validation of SB7 itself; its plausibility borrows from narrative-persuasion research (Green & Brock, above) that it does not cite or test against. A reported "StoryBrand 2.0" edition (2025) was not verified in this pass.

### Stengel (2011) — *Grow*
- Jim Stengel. *Grow: How Ideals Power Growth and Profit at the World's Greatest Companies*. Crown Business, 2011. ISBN-13: `9780307720351`. (Note: the title contains no "50" — the "Stengel 50" is the study inside the book.)
- Metadata verified via: https://crownpublishing.com/archives/15428
- Supports: nothing evidentially — included because the "Stengel 50" is the most-cited quantitative claim for brand purpose and has been specifically refuted.
- Limits: documented refutations — Richard Shotton (Mediatel 2015; Medium, "Why was Jim Stengel's Grow so popular when it's so flawed?"; The Drum, "Truthiness in marketing", Apr 2017) showed survivorship/selection problems: the 50 were picked ex post from Millward Brown's BrandZ database, many were private firms or sub-brands, and only 9 of the 26 investable stocks beat the S&P 500. Ehrenberg-Bass published "The flawed Stengel Study of Business Growth" (marketingscience.info); Mark Ritson has repeatedly attacked its use as purpose evidence in *Marketing Week*.
