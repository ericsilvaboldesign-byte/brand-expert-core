# Distinctive Brand Assets — Primary-Evidence Briefing

Primary theorist: **Prof. Jenni Romaniuk, Ehrenberg-Bass Institute (EBI)**,
University of South Australia. Core text: *Building Distinctive Brand Assets*,
Oxford University Press, 2018 (ISBN `9780190311506`).

**Access disclosure.** Direct page fetching is blocked in this research
environment for all external domains (including marketingscience.info and
oup.com). Every claim below was verified from **search-retrieved content
excerpts of the named real pages** (access level: excerpt-accessed throughout;
abstract-level for the academic papers). Nothing is presented from memory
alone; unconfirmable points are flagged.

## 1. Definition

Distinctive Assets are **"the non-brand name elements that trigger the brand
into the memory of category buyers"** — colours, logos, characters, sounds,
slogans, typefaces (EBI wording, attributed to Romaniuk 2016/2018 and Sharp &
Romaniuk 2010; the same definition opens the 2026 *International Journal of
Advertising* benchmarking paper). They work by building memory structures so
the brand is identified and retrieved **even without the brand name present**.

## 2. Fame

**Fame = the % of category buyers who link the asset to the brand** — the
reach of the memory link across the category-buying population (not just the
brand's customers). EBI describes it as "the percentage of category buyers'
brains where the brand has a salient link to the asset." A common working
benchmark: ~50% Fame before an asset can reliably do branding work solo.
(Threshold confirmed only in derived/practitioner explainers, not from
Romaniuk's own text — flagged.)

## 3. Uniqueness

**Uniqueness = of the people who link the asset to any brand, the % who link
it ONLY to this brand** — the target brand's share of all brand-to-asset
linkages. It quantifies competitive ownership; 100% is the ideal, ~50% a
common minimum working threshold (same flag as above). The 2026 IJA paper's
formulation: "how exclusively it is linked to the brand."

## 4. Asset strength — the Distinctive Asset Grid

Romaniuk (2018) plots **Fame (y) × Uniqueness (x)** in a 2×2 grid. Quadrant
names confirmed verbatim across multiple accessed explainers and a published
figure reproduction ("Distinctive Asset grid from Romaniuk (2018)"):

| Quadrant | Fame | Uniqueness | Prescription |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Use or Lose** | High | High | Goal state; strong enough to use solo (can even stand in for the logo). Keep using it or it decays. |
| **Investment Potential** | Low | High | A hidden gem: genuinely yours, not yet famous — invest, repeat, build fame. The realistic starting quadrant for new/small brands. |
| **Avoid Solo Use** | High | Low | Famous but shared/generic (accessed example: Ferrari's racing red) — never deploy without the brand name attached. |
| **Ignore or Test** | Low | Low | Weak or background; drop, rework, or test only if the element is early in development. |

The grid is an **ongoing diagnostic**, aiming to move selected assets to the
top-right over time. (An earlier EBI formulation used "Prevalence" for the
fame axis.)

## 5. Asset ownership

Per accessed Romaniuk interviews and book-derived material:

- Building an asset requires **reach** (enough category buyers exposed),
  **co-presentation** (the asset repeatedly shown *together with the brand
  name* so the link forms), and **consistency** (same execution over time).
- Ownership is a **memory outcome, not a legal one**: an element crosses into
  ownership only after consistent use builds the link in enough buyers'
  memories (the Tiffany-blue / Cadbury-purple pattern).
- **Focus**: building many assets simultaneously fails — "trying to build many
  assets at the same time leads to a lack of executional focus" (Romaniuk,
  accessed excerpt).
- **Longevity discipline**: "make 'no' your default response to change — if
  you select smartly, your brand's Distinctive Assets should outlast you"
  (Romaniuk interview excerpt).
- *Not confirmed:* any specific number of years required for ownership — no
  fixed period appeared in any accessed material; the guidance is
  consistency + reach + co-presentation until the metrics clear thresholds.

## 6. Asset types

Romaniuk (2018) classifies assets into **visual, audio, and word-based**
families (confirmed via a published figure reproduction, EBI pages, and the
book's table of contents in excerpts):

- **Visual**: shapes (logos, pack shapes), colours, faces/characters,
  words-as-visuals, **fonts**, and "moments".
- **Audio**: five sound types — non-vocal cues (the Intel bong), vocal cues,
  jingles (McDonald's "ba da ba ba baa"), brand music, rhythmic patterns
  (Mastercard's sonic heartbeat).
- **Word-based**: taglines and other words/phrases.
- **People**: spokespeople/celebrities (book chapter "The celebrity dilemma"),
  plus advertising-style assets.

## 7. Portfolio cohesion — the Distinctive Asset palette

- The book's Part 3 introduces a **Distinctive Asset palette** and a
  management system acting as "an early warning system to identify potential
  threats" (publisher description, accessed excerpt).
- Romaniuk's heuristic: **"think menu rather than meal"** — build a set of
  assets and choose the best one per context rather than deploying everything
  everywhere; evaluate assets as a set across all branding contexts.
- A "4–5 assets across different sensory categories" rule of thumb appears
  only in secondary summaries — flagged as secondary. The term "anchor asset"
  could not be confirmed in any accessed material — flagged as unconfirmed
  terminology.

## 8. What "distinctive" does NOT mean

**Distinctiveness ≠ differentiation.**

- **Differentiation** = being perceived as *different or better* on a valued
  attribute (a positioning claim).
- **Distinctiveness** = being *identifiable as yourself* — unique memory
  triggers, no meaning required.

Foundational paper: **Romaniuk, Sharp & Ehrenberg (2007)**, "Evidence
concerning the importance of perceived brand differentiation", *Australasian
Marketing Journal* 15(2), 42–54, DOI `10.1016/S1441-3582(07)70042-3`
(abstract-accessed): across many categories and two countries, competing
brands are perceived as barely differentiated yet keep being bought — placing
distinctiveness, not differentiation, at the centre of brand strategy.
Corollary (Romaniuk in *Marketing Week*, title/summary-level access only —
flagged): loading assets with persuasive *meaning* is a different job that
can undermine their identification role.

## 9. What the research CAN and CANNOT tell a designer

**CAN (grounded in accessed material):**
- **Measure** any existing or candidate element's branding strength in
  buyers' memories (Fame %, Uniqueness %) and locate it on the grid.
- **Prioritize investment**: push Investment Potential assets, pair
  Avoid-Solo-Use assets with the name, rely on Use-or-Lose assets.
- **Benchmark by type**: characters/logos/logotypes show the greatest
  ownership potential and colour the least (Ward et al. 2020); shape-based
  assets benchmark strongest (≈40% Fame / 71% Uniqueness) and colour weakest
  (≈12% / 39%) across 1,162 assets, 21 categories, 4 countries, 9 years
  (IJA 2026 abstract).
- **Enforce discipline**: consistency, co-presentation with the name, few
  assets at a time, default-"no" to redesign.

**CANNOT:**
- **Choose or judge a new asset aesthetically**: "there are no best assets,
  but there are definitely worse ones" (Romaniuk, accessed interview
  excerpt); Ward et al. (2020) find competitive intensity "varies for
  elements of the same type, suggesting that practitioner execution plays a
  critical role" — type-level data cannot pick a specific design.
- **Predict a new asset's future**: Fame/Uniqueness quantify memory links
  that already exist from past exposure; a brand-new asset necessarily starts
  low-Fame and is unscoreable beyond "test". *(The "retrospective" framing is
  analyst synthesis from the grid mechanics — flagged.)*
- **Substitute for creative judgment**: originality, likability, and craft
  quality remain design decisions outside the measurement system.
