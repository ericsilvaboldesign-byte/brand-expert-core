# Knowledge Map — What Branding Knowledge Is Supported, By What, With What Limits

Claims are graded with the authority scale in [`README.md`](README.md)
(A1 peer-reviewed empirical · A2 scholarly conceptual · B industry-empirical ·
C practitioner canon · D popular/opinion). Full citations and verification
evidence for every source are in [`SOURCES.md`](SOURCES.md). Each claim states
its **status**: *established* (within limits), *framework* (coherent and
teachable, not a tested finding), *craft* (professional consensus about
process), *contested* (credible sources disagree), or *unsupported*.

## 1. How brands grow

**Claim: Smaller brands have both fewer buyers and slightly lower loyalty (double jeopardy).**
- Supported by: Ehrenberg, Goodhardt & Barwise 1990 (A1); replicated across categories and countries in the Ehrenberg-Bass corpus (B).
- Status: **established** — one of marketing's few law-like regularities.
- Limits: cross-sectional and stationary-market; documented exceptions (niche, change-of-pace brands); Fader & Schmittlein 1993 found high-share brands hold *excess* loyalty beyond the baseline. It describes market structure; it does not by itself prescribe growth tactics.

**Claim: Brand growth comes overwhelmingly from increasing penetration (more buyers), not from deepening loyalty.**
- Supported by: Sharp 2010, Romaniuk & Sharp 2015 (B), resting on the A1 Ehrenberg substrate.
- Status: **established within its evidence base**, prescriptively contested.
- Limits: built on Ehrenberg-Bass proprietary panel data, mostly repertoire CPG-like categories; the extension to services, luxury, and B2B (HBG Part 2) got a mixed academic review (Wilson, IJMR 2016). The descriptive pattern is much stronger than the strategic prohibitions ("don't target", "don't differentiate") derived from it — see §4.

**Claim: Brands grow by building mental availability (being thought of in more buying situations) and physical availability (being easy to find and buy).**
- Supported by: Sharp 2010; Romaniuk & Sharp 2015 (B).
- Status: **established as an organizing account** of the penetration finding; the constructs are Ehrenberg-Bass measurement inventions.
- Limits: measurement of mental availability (category entry points) is largely proprietary; independent replication outside the institute is thin.

## 2. Advertising and brand-building effectiveness

**Claim: Advertising produces real sales effects, but more weight alone often does nothing; creative/strategy changes matter more.**
- Supported by: Lodish et al. 1995 meta-analysis of 389 split-cable experiments (A1).
- Status: **established** for its domain.
- Limits: US packaged goods, 1980s TV; formally challenged in part by Riskey (JMR 1997); media environment has changed (partial update: Hu, Lodish & Krieger 2007).

**Claim: Long-term brand building and short-term activation are different mechanisms; sustained growth needs both, weighted toward brand (~60/40 as a starting heuristic).**
- Supported by: Binet & Field 2013 (B).
- Status: **established as a robust pattern in the IPA databank; heuristic, not law.**
- Limits: the databank is self-selected award entries from large B2C advertisers (selection bias — Guild/BBH critique, acknowledged by Binet); cases largely predate performance-native media; the authors themselves frame 60/40 as context-dependent, and it is routinely over-applied.

## 3. Brand equity

**Claim: Brand equity is the differential effect of brand knowledge (awareness + associations/image) on customer response to marketing.**
- Supported by: Keller 1993 (A2); Aaker 1991's five-asset taxonomy (A2); consolidated in Keller & Swaminathan's textbook (A2).
- Status: **framework** — the field's shared vocabulary, not a tested causal model.
- Limits: no standardized measurement (Aaker's assets, Keller's pyramid, and commercial systems like BrandZ/Interbrand disagree); the frameworks are compatible with almost any observed outcome, which is why they persist and why they prove little.

## 4. Positioning: differentiation vs distinctiveness — the field's live contradiction

**Claim (differentiation school): Winning brands occupy a distinct position in the prospect's mind; strategy means owning an idea competitors don't, and radical differentiation outperforms.**
- Supported by: Ries & Trout 1981 (C); Neumeier 2006 (C); conceptually elaborated in Keller's brand-image apparatus (A2); defended with data arguments by Hollis, HBR 2013.
- Limits: the canon's evidence is anecdote; its psychological claims are stylized rather than cited.

**Claim (distinctiveness school): Differentiation is weak and largely irrelevant; brands should instead build distinctive assets (recognizable codes) and availability, because buyers satisfice among functionally similar options.**
- Supported by: Sharp 2010, Romaniuk & Sharp 2015, Romaniuk 2018 (B).
- Limits: proprietary data; the prescriptive leap from cross-sectional regularities is contested (Ritson's Marketing Week columns and the 2017 Festival of Marketing debate; Hollis's meaningful-difference counter-case; Sharp's published reply "Answering critics" 2016).

**Status: CONTESTED — genuinely unresolved.** This is the most important honest finding in the corpus: the two positions ground most working brand strategy, they conflict at the level of prescription, and no source in the register settles it. What *is* common ground: distinctive, consistently used brand identifiers are valuable (both schools), and being remembered matters more than being unique in attribute terms than the differentiation canon assumed.

## 5. Brand personality

**Claim: Consumers reliably ascribe human-like trait structures to brands, and this can be measured.**
- Supported by: Aaker 1997 (A1).
- Status: **established core finding; the specific five-dimension scale is contested.**
- Limits: construct critique by Azoulay & Kapferer 2003 (A2 — the scale mixes identity facets with personality); five factors failed full cross-cultural replication (Aaker, Benet-Martínez & Garolera 2001) and within-category application (Austin et al. 2003); a replacement scale exists (Geuens et al. 2009). Practical upshot: brand-personality language is usable, but treating Aaker's five dimensions as universal measurement is not supported.

## 6. Visual identity and design response

**Claim: Easily processed stimuli (simple, clear, symmetrical, prototypical) are liked more — the mechanism behind "clean" design preference.**
- Supported by: Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman 2004 (A1 review).
- Limits: not universal — preference for complexity/novelty under interest motives (Graf & Landwehr 2015); fluency effects can be discounted when attributed externally.

**Claim: Familiarity itself breeds liking (mere exposure) — repetition builds preference for brand assets.**
- Supported by: Zajonc 1968 (A1); Bornstein 1989 meta-analysis.
- Limits: inverted-U — overexposure reduces liking (Bornstein 1989; Montoya et al. 2017); strongest for brief exposure to initially neutral stimuli, narrower than typical campaign exposure.

**Claim: Specific logo design properties (elaborateness, naturalness, harmony) measurably drive recognition, affect, and shared meaning.**
- Supported by: Henderson & Cote 1998 (A1).
- Limits: unfamiliar logos, US lab sample; only partial cross-cultural generalization (Henderson et al. 2003; Chilean validation 2020). Guidelines inform choices; they do not guarantee market outcomes.

**Claim: Brands should build and consistently deploy distinctive assets (colors, characters, shapes, sonic cues), managed for fame and uniqueness.**
- Supported by: Romaniuk 2018 (B); consonant with the fluency/exposure mechanisms above (A1).
- Status: **established direction of practice**; measurement specifics are proprietary.
- Limits: sits inside the contested Sharp framework (§4), though this particular prescription is the part both schools accept.

## 7. Naming

**Claim: The sound of a name systematically conveys attribute meaning (front vowels → small/fast/light; certain consonants → sharpness etc.).**
- Supported by: Klink 2000 (A1).
- Status: **established in the lab.**
- Limits: fictitious names, forced choice, perception not purchase; effects moderated by category relevance and valence (Lowrey & Shrum 2007) and language (Shrum et al. 2012; Klink & Athaide 2012). A naming input, not a success predictor.

## 8. Brand storytelling and messaging

**Claim: Narrative absorption ("transportation") reduces counterarguing and shifts beliefs — stories persuade differently than arguments.**
- Supported by: Green & Brock 2000 (A1); Van Laer et al. 2014 meta-analysis (moderate average effects).
- Status: **established, with moderate and moderated effects.**
- Limits: partly correlational original evidence; effects vary with storyteller, story, audience.

**Claim: The SB7 formula (customer as hero, brand as guide) is a proven messaging method.**
- Supported by: Miller 2017 (D) only.
- Status: **unsupported as stated.** It is a plausible craft template consistent with narrative-persuasion research it neither cites nor tests; no published validation of the formula itself exists in this corpus.

## 9. Brand purpose

**Claim: Purpose-/ideal-driven brands grow faster ("start with why"; the Stengel 50).**
- Supported by: Sinek 2009 (D); Stengel 2011 (D).
- Status: **unsupported — and the quantitative flagship is refuted.** The Stengel 50 analysis suffers survivorship/selection bias and circularity, documented by Shotton (Mediatel 2015; Medium; The Drum 2017: only 9 of the 26 investable Stengel stocks beat the S&P 500), Ehrenberg-Bass ("The flawed Stengel Study"), and Ritson (Marketing Week). Sinek's limbic-brain framing is not supported by neuroscience.
- Honest formulation the corpus *does* support: purpose can be an internal alignment and creative device (C-level craft claim); there is no verified evidence here that purpose causes growth.

## 10. Cultural branding

**Claim: Iconic brands succeed by performing identity myths that resolve cultural tensions of their moment — a mechanism distinct from mindshare/associations models.**
- Supported by: Holt 2004 (A2).
- Status: **framework** — the most rigorous cultural account available, built on retrospective case interpretation.
- Limits: no predictive validation; applies to the rare iconic tier, not everyday brand management.

## 11. Identity systems and the design process

**Claim: Brand identity is usefully organized as a structured system (Aaker's identity model; Kapferer's prism: physique, personality, culture, relationship, reflection, self-image).**
- Supported by: Aaker 1996, Kapferer 2004/2012 (A2).
- Status: **framework.** Competing vocabularies with no empirical way to choose between them; value is in forcing explicit, shared decisions.

**Claim: Professional identity development follows a phased process — research/investigation → strategy → design → implementation/touchpoints → governance/asset management.**
- Supported by: Wheeler 2003–2024, Johnson 2016, Olins 2003, Neumeier 2003 (C — independent convergence of leading practitioners).
- Status: **craft** — the strongest thing practitioner consensus can establish: how competent work is organized, deliverables, sequencing, governance.
- Limits: no controlled comparison against alternative processes; case libraries skew to celebrated programs (survivorship). Craft consensus is reliable about *process quality*, silent about *causal market outcomes*.

## What this corpus does NOT establish

Stated so downstream use cannot silently over-claim:

- **No causal, generalizable link from any identity/equity framework to business results.** The A2 layer is vocabulary, not verified mechanism.
- **No resolution of differentiation vs distinctiveness** (§4). Downstream advice must either present both or state which school it is following.
- **No validated formulas**: SB7, the Golden Circle, 60/40-as-law, and the Stengel 50 are respectively unvalidated, unsupported, over-applied heuristic, and refuted.
- **Nothing here verifies popular design-psychology folklore** (e.g., "color increases brand recognition by 80%", golden-ratio logo superiority, "consumers decide in X seconds") — none of it entered the register because no verifiable authoritative source was found for it in this pass.
- **Lab-bounded findings stay lab-bounded**: logo properties, sound symbolism, fluency, and mere exposure are real mechanisms with unknown effect sizes in any specific market application.
